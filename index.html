Part One — The Mindset

The hardest part of this decision has nothing to do with the business model. It has everything to do with whether you've made up your mind to build something of your own — whether that means walking away from a job on day one, or working toward it deliberately while you're still employed.

Question 1
Where are you on the path away from employment?

You don't have to have already quit to be a strong fit here. Some of our best franchisees built their DOXA business alongside a full-time job before making the leap – that's a legitimate path, not a lesser one, as long as it's a real plan and not a someday.

So think this through honestly: if a recruiter called you tomorrow with a strong offer – good title, solid comp, interesting company – what would you do? Do you know roughly when, and under what conditions, you'd go full-time in the business? If you're planning to build this alongside your current job for a while, do you know how much time you can realistically protect for it each week?

The candidates who succeed here – whether they start part-time or jump in with both feet – have already answered these questions honestly. They know where they stand and what has to be true for that to change. If you're still hedging with no plan for how or when that changes, that's worth naming here too.

Question 2
What's actually driving your desire for business ownership?

Skip the polished answer. Is it autonomy? Income ceiling? Legacy? A specific frustration with where you've been? Something you're trying to build?

The sharper your answer, the more resilient you'll be in those early months when the business is still taking shape and the results haven't shown up yet – whether you're building it full-time or squeezing it in around a day job.

Your answer
Question 3
Are you comfortable being early?

The franchisees who thrive inside an emerging brand like DOXA see “early” as an advantage. They want direct access to the people running the company. They have opinions about what's working, and they expect those opinions to carry weight. They're building something – they're not just operating something.

If that sounds like you, these should be straightforward:

When I've been part of something early-stage, my instinct was to lean in and help shape it – not wait for someone to hand me a playbook.
Having a direct voice with leadership matters more to me than having a fully documented system to follow.
I can work through ambiguity, knowing my experience and feedback will help improve the process for everyone who comes after me.

Part Two — The Reality Check

This is where it gets practical. Building a DOXA franchise requires consistent, real effort – especially in the first 6 to 12 months. The go-to-market strategy is relationship-based: networking events, coffee meetings, activating your professional contacts. Whether that time comes from a full week or from early mornings, evenings, and weekends around a current job, it has to actually be on your calendar. It doesn't happen in leftover hours.

Question 4
Do you have the financial runway – and the time – mapped out?

We mean actually mapped out – living expenses, personal obligations, plus the investment itself.

Write out the reality: what do you need monthly to cover your life? How many months can you sustain that without income from the business? If you're still employed while you build this, your math looks a little different – but map it out anyway: what income does the business eventually need to replace, and roughly when?

Also be honest about time: if you're not yet full-time in the business, how many real hours per week can you protect for it – not hoped-for hours, actual ones already carved out of your schedule?

The franchisees who've built the strongest businesses went in with these numbers mapped out and their eyes open. They didn't assume the business would ramp faster than it did – and they weren't caught off guard when it didn't.

Question 5
What does your support system look like?

Is your partner, spouse, or family genuinely behind this? We're talking truly behind it – not tolerating it. Do the people closest to you understand what the first year looks like, and are they in?

If you're building this while still employed, that also means late nights and weekends spent on the business instead of somewhere else. Does your household understand that trade, and are they on board with it for as long as it takes?

Part Three — The Honest Question

This is the one most people skip. Work through it anyway.

Question 6
What's been in the way?

Most first-time entrepreneurs don't lack the skills or the drive. What slows them down is FUD: fear, uncertainty, and doubt. It shows up right when things get real, and it follows a remarkably consistent pattern across people who eventually build great businesses – full-time from day one or gradually alongside a job.

Which of these have kept you from owning a business before now? Select all that apply.

Fear of failure and what it would mean for how others see me
Uncertainty about whether I can replace my current income
Doubt that I have what it takes to sell or build a client base
Gaps in my confidence around running a business operationally
Not sure how to build this around a job I'm not ready to leave yet
Concern that the timing isn't right in my personal life
Worry about what happens if I invest and it doesn't work out
